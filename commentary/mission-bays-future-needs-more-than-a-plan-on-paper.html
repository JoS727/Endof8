Mission Bay’s Future Needs More Than a Plan on Paper

Local news and commentary by Joseph Calitoy
November 17, 2026

Mission Bay’s future is back before City Hall. Residents deserve a clear explanation of what this decision changes and what still has to happen before they see work on the ground.

The San Diego City Council’s November 17 agenda lists consideration of California Coastal Commission modifications to De Anza Natural as Item 330 in the afternoon session scheduled to begin at 2 p.m. The City describes a framework intended to guide land uses over the next 20 to 30 years. This commentary does not report a verified outcome of that hearing. Official Council agenda · City meeting preview

That is a long commitment to a place the public should be able to understand without becoming land-use specialists.

De Anza Cove sits in the northeast corner of Mission Bay. The City’s plans combine wetland restoration with recreation and lower-cost overnight access to the coast. Its project description includes a waterfront trail, non-motorized boating, camping and recreational vehicle facilities. These are planned uses, not an announcement that new facilities are open. City project overview

The different uses deserve serious consideration. Restoring habitat matters. So does keeping coastal recreation within reach of people who cannot afford a waterfront hotel. The public should be able to see how those goals fit together, where they compete, and what the final decisions require.

There is already a history here. The Council unanimously approved the De Anza Natural amendment in May 2024. The City identifies expanded wetlands, improved water quality and preparation for rising sea levels among the plan’s goals. Its public process also includes conservation, recreation, boating and camping interests. City planning record

Now comes the harder question: how does an approved vision become something residents can use and a habitat that actually functions?

The proposed November 17 resolution itself distinguishes Council adoption from the next step: it would not take effect until the Coastal Commission unconditionally certifies the provisions as a local coastal program amendment. That condition is not evidence that certification has occurred. Proposed action, Item 330

The City’s August 2024 fact sheet lays out several separate stages. Following final coastal certification, a General Development Plan process is intended to turn concepts into a preferred design, cost estimates and schedules. Construction documents, permits and funding follow. Those stages should remain visible in every progress update. A policy decision should never be presented as though it also supplied a construction budget or an opening date. City implementation fact sheet

For Ocean Beach, Point Loma and the rest of San Diego, useful accountability would be straightforward:

Publish the adopted changes in plain language. Identify the next decision and the department responsible for it. Separate money secured from money still being sought. Explain how existing recreation and public access will be handled through each phase. Give residents a way to follow restoration results after construction, not just the construction itself.

These are practical expectations for a project meant to shape decades of public use.

Mission Bay belongs in the everyday life of this city. The measure of De Anza Natural will be healthier habitat, accessible recreation and a public that can see whether the promises are being kept.

Reporting note: This commentary addresses the November 17, 2026 agenda and published planning documents. A completed vote, final coastal certification, funded construction schedule and completed restoration were not independently verified before publication. The City Clerk’s meeting records are the source for subsequent official action.
