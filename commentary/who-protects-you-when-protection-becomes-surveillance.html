Who Protects You When Protection Becomes Surveillance?

Commentary by Joseph Calitoy
November 13, 2026

A person who calls for help should be able to understand what happens to their information afterward. That should be a basic expectation.

Voice of San Diego reports that San Diego police searched Flock license-plate records for an officer and a domestic violence survivor while investigating alleged misconduct involving their relationship. The department says the searches were permitted. A city privacy-board leader questions whether they complied with the rules.

That disagreement deserves a public answer. If the people responsible for oversight and the people using the system disagree about its limits, residents cannot reasonably be expected to know where those limits are.

Investigate officer misconduct. Absolutely. But explain why a survivor’s movements need to become part of that investigation, who approves that access, and who reviews the decision afterward.

There is another detail worth keeping straight: according to the reporting, the Flock search did not produce the evidence investigators sought. Patrol-car location records did.

We should be able to demand accountability without treating every available surveillance tool as automatically justified.

Before expanding systems like this, show the public enforceable limits. Explain what makes a search necessary. Keep records of who accessed the information and why. Give independent reviewers enough access to catch misuse, and make consequences real.

Calling something a public-safety tool does not settle whether every use of it serves the public.

The people being watched deserve more than an assurance from the people doing the watching.

Based on reporting by Jakob McWhinney for Voice of San Diego. Read the original investigation at the link above.
